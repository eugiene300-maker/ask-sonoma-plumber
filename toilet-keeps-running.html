Why Does My Toilet Keep Running?

CL Reviewed by Cody Lehman · Updated August 2026

[ Pipes, Pressure & Fixtures · Sonoma County ]
Answer

A toilet that keeps running is almost always caused by something in the tank not sealing or shutting off: a worn or misaligned flapper, a chain that is too short or too long, a float set too high, or a fill valve that will not stop. The good news is that most running toilets are an easy, low-cost DIY fix, usually a new flapper. Start with a dye test to confirm where the water is escaping, then replace the failed part.

Most likely causes

  1. Worn or warped flapper

    The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank. Over time it gets stiff, warped, or mineral-crusted (common with Sonoma County hard water) and no longer seals against the flush valve, so water trickles from tank to bowl continuously. This is the single most common cause.

  2. Chain too short or too long

    The chain connects the flush handle to the flapper. If it is too short it holds the flapper slightly open; if it is too long it can slip under the flapper and prop it up. Either way the seal is broken and the toilet runs.

  3. Float set too high

    If the float (a ball on an arm or a cup on the fill valve) is set too high, the water rises above the overflow tube and drains into the bowl nonstop, so the fill valve never shuts off. Lowering the float fixes it.

  4. Fill valve failure

    The fill valve refills the tank after a flush and shuts off when the water reaches the set level. A worn or clogged fill valve may fail to shut off completely, causing a constant hiss or intermittent refilling. Hard-water sediment accelerates this wear.

  5. Leaking or cracked flush valve

    The flush valve is the plastic seat the flapper closes against. If it is cracked, pitted, or scaled with mineral deposits, even a new flapper cannot seal. This is less common and usually requires replacing the flush valve or the whole tank kit.

Safe DIY fixes for a running toilet

  1. Run the dye test: put a few drops of food coloring or a dye tablet in the tank, wait 10 to 15 minutes without flushing, and check the bowl. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper or flush valve is leaking.
  2. Remove the tank lid and set it flat on a towel where it cannot fall. Flush and watch what happens as the tank empties and refills.
  3. Check the chain: it should have just a little slack (about a half inch) when the flapper is closed. Reposition it on the flush handle arm or trim excess links so it neither props the flapper open nor slips under it.
  4. Inspect the flapper: press it gently onto the seat. If it is stiff, cracked, warped, or coated with mineral scale, replace it. Turn off the shutoff valve behind the toilet, flush to empty the tank, unclip the old flapper, and clip on a matching new one. Universal flappers are a few dollars at any hardware store.
  5. Adjust the float: for a cup-style float, pinch the clip and slide it down the rod to lower the water level about an inch below the top of the overflow tube. For a ball-and-arm float, turn the adjustment screw or gently bend the arm down.
  6. Wipe the flush valve seat clean of any grit or mineral buildup so the new flapper can seal, then turn the water back on and run the dye test again to confirm the leak is gone.

When to call a licensed plumber

Call a plumber if the running continues after replacing the flapper and adjusting the float, if the flush valve or overflow tube is cracked, if water is leaking onto the floor from the tank bolts or supply line, or if the toilet is old and repairs are adding up. A pro can rebuild the tank or advise on replacement.

How to prevent it

  • Replace the flapper every 4 to 5 years as routine maintenance, sooner with our hard water.
  • Do not use in-tank drop-in bleach tablets; they degrade rubber flappers and seals quickly.
  • Wipe mineral scale off the flush valve seat when you change a flapper.
  • Run a dye test once or twice a year to catch silent leaks early.
  • Consider a whole-home water softener or conditioner to reduce scale on all fixtures.

Frequently asked questions

How much water does a running toilet waste?

A steadily running toilet can waste hundreds of gallons a day, and the EPA notes that household leaks (toilets are the biggest culprit) waste nearly 1 trillion gallons nationwide each year. A single running toilet can add many dollars to your monthly water bill, so fixing it usually pays for itself fast.

Why does my toilet run for a few seconds every once in a while on its own?

That phantom or ghost flushing means water is slowly leaking past the flapper, dropping the tank level until the fill valve briefly kicks on to top it off. It almost always means the flapper is not sealing and should be replaced.

Is a running toilet an emergency?

No. It is not a flooding risk because the excess water drains into the bowl and down the drain. It is a water-waste and cost issue, so fix it soon, but it will not damage your home the way a burst pipe would.
