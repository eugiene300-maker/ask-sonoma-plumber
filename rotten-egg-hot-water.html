Why Does My Hot Water Smell Like Rotten Eggs?

CL Reviewed by Cody Lehman · Updated August 2026

[ Water Heaters · Sonoma County ]
Answer

A rotten-egg smell from your hot water is hydrogen sulfide gas, usually produced when sulfate-reducing bacteria in the water heater react with the tank's sacrificial anode rod. If the smell is only on the hot side, the water heater is almost always the source, and the fix is to flush and disinfect the tank and swap the standard magnesium anode for an aluminum/zinc or a powered anode. If both hot and cold water smell, the problem is more likely in the well or water supply and needs different treatment.

Most likely causes

  1. Sulfate-reducing bacteria in the tank

    Harmless-to-drink but odor-causing bacteria can grow inside a water heater, especially on well water. They feed on sulfates and release hydrogen sulfide gas, the classic rotten-egg smell.

  2. Reaction with the anode rod

    Water heaters contain a sacrificial magnesium anode rod that protects the tank from corrosion. It can react with those bacteria and the water chemistry to accelerate hydrogen sulfide production, which is why the odor is often worst in hot water.

  3. Well water sulfur

    Some Sonoma County wells naturally carry sulfur and sulfates. This gives the bacteria more to work with and makes the odor more likely and more persistent.

  4. A tank that sits idle or runs cool

    Water heaters that sit unused (vacation homes) or are set to low temperatures give bacteria a comfortable environment to multiply, intensifying the smell.

How to fix rotten-egg hot water

  1. Confirm the source: run cold water alone and smell it, then run hot alone. If only the hot smells, it is the water heater.
  2. Turn off the water heater (set gas to pilot or switch off electric power) and shut the cold-water inlet.
  3. Drain and flush the tank to clear sediment and bacteria-laden water.
  4. Disinfect the tank by adding hydrogen peroxide or a chlorine solution, letting it sit, then flushing thoroughly per the manufacturer's guidance.
  5. Replace the standard magnesium anode rod with an aluminum/zinc alloy rod or a powered (impressed-current) anode, which resist the reaction that produces the odor.
  6. If the smell returns or is present on cold water too, test the well and consider whole-home treatment such as chlorination or aeration.

How to prevent it

  • Keep the water heater at a proper temperature (around 120F for daily use); very low settings encourage bacteria.
  • Flush the tank annually to clear sediment that harbors bacteria.
  • On well water with sulfur, consider a powered anode from the start.
  • If you leave a home vacant for long periods, flush and run the hot water before regular use.
  • Have your well water tested periodically so sulfur and bacteria issues are caught early.

Frequently asked questions

Is rotten-egg water dangerous to drink?

The hydrogen sulfide levels typical in household water are generally an odor and nuisance problem rather than an immediate health hazard, but the smell is unpleasant and can indicate bacterial activity. If your water is from a private well, test it to be sure nothing else is going on.

Why is it only the hot water that smells?

Heat and the water heater's anode rod together create ideal conditions for sulfate-reducing bacteria to produce hydrogen sulfide. Cold water does not pass through the tank, so when only the hot side smells, the heater is the source.

Should I just remove the anode rod?

No. Removing the anode entirely stops the smell but leaves the tank unprotected from corrosion, which shortens its life and can void the warranty. Replacing it with an aluminum/zinc or powered anode is the correct fix.

How do I know if it is the well and not the heater?

Smell the cold water on its own. If the cold water also smells of sulfur, the source is the well or supply, not the heater, and you will need whole-home treatment like chlorination or aeration rather than just an anode swap.
