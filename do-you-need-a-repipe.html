Do You Need a Repipe? Signs to Watch

CL Reviewed by Cody Lehman · Updated August 2026

[ Costs & Decisions · Sonoma County ]
Answer

You likely need a whole-home repipe if you have repeated pinhole leaks, rusty or brown water from multiple faucets, chronic low pressure, or confirmed galvanized steel or polybutylene pipe, especially in a home that is 40-plus years old. A single isolated leak is usually a repair; a pattern of failures across the house is the signal to repipe. When two or more of these signs show up together, it is time to have a licensed plumber evaluate the whole system.

Most likely causes

  1. Repeated pinhole leaks

    One leak can be a fluke. Several pinhole leaks in a year, or leaks appearing in different parts of the house, point to pipe that is failing throughout, not in one spot.

  2. Rusty, brown, or metallic water

    Discolored water, especially from more than one fixture, usually means galvanized steel pipe is corroding from the inside. This is a strong repipe indicator.

  3. Chronic low water pressure

    Galvanized pipe narrows as internal corrosion and scale build up, choking flow. If pressure has slowly declined across the whole home, the pipe interior is likely the cause.

  4. Galvanized or polybutylene pipe

    Galvanized steel (common in pre-1970s homes) corrodes and rusts. Polybutylene (used roughly 1978-1995) is prone to sudden failure. Confirming either material is often reason enough to plan a repipe.

  5. Home age

    Pipe has a service life. Homes past the 40-to-50-year mark on original galvanized plumbing are living on borrowed time, even if leaks have not started yet.

A simple decision rule

  1. Identify your pipe material: check exposed pipe in the garage, crawlspace, or under sinks, or ask a plumber to confirm.
  2. Count recent leaks: two or more in the past year across different areas leans strongly toward repipe.
  3. Check your water color and pressure at several fixtures, not just one.
  4. Apply the rule: if you have galvanized or polybutylene pipe PLUS any one symptom (leaks, discoloration, or low pressure), plan a repipe rather than another patch.
  5. For copper systems with a single isolated leak and otherwise good water and pressure, a targeted repair is usually enough.

When to call a licensed plumber

Call a licensed plumber when leaks are recurring, water is discolored, pressure has dropped house-wide, or you have confirmed galvanized or polybutylene pipe. A pro can scope the system, confirm the material, and tell you whether a repair or a full repipe is the smarter spend.

How to prevent it

  • Learn your pipe material now so a future leak is not a surprise.
  • Address the first pinhole leak with a plumber's opinion on overall pipe health, not just a patch.
  • Keep household pressure moderate with a pressure-reducing valve to reduce stress on aging pipe.
  • If you soften or condition your water, you slow the scale that shortens pipe and appliance life.

Frequently asked questions

How do I tell galvanized from copper pipe?

Galvanized steel is dull gray, magnetic, and often shows rust at threaded joints. Copper is reddish-brown and non-magnetic. Polybutylene is usually gray, blue, or black flexible plastic with crimped fittings. A magnet and a quick look under a sink often tell the story.

Can I repipe just part of the house?

Sometimes. If failures are limited to one branch or the material is mixed, a partial repipe can make sense. But if the whole home is on failing galvanized pipe, a full repipe usually costs less over time than piecemeal repairs.

Is brown water dangerous?

Occasional rusty water from corroding pipe is usually an aesthetic and plumbing problem rather than an immediate health emergency, but persistent discoloration should be checked. If you are on a private well, test the water to rule out other sources.

Does a repipe add home value?

New PEX or copper plumbing removes a major buyer concern, especially in older North Bay homes, and is a documented, permitted upgrade that supports resale.
