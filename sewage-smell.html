What Causes a Sewage Smell Indoors?

CL Reviewed by Cody Lehman · Updated August 2026

[ Drains & Sewer · Sonoma County ]
Answer

A sewage or rotten-egg smell indoors means sewer gas is escaping into your home instead of venting out the roof. The most common cause is a dried-out P-trap under a rarely used sink, shower, or floor drain, which lets gas rise up the pipe. Other causes are a failed wax ring under a toilet, a blocked or damaged plumbing vent, a cracked drain or sewer lateral, or a problem sump/ejector pit. Sewer gas contains methane and hydrogen sulfide, so while low levels are mainly a nuisance, you should find and fix the source promptly.

Most likely causes

  1. Dry P-trap (unused drain)

    Every drain has a U-shaped trap that holds water to block sewer gas. In a guest bath, floor drain, or seldom-used fixture, that water evaporates over weeks and the seal is lost, letting gas straight into the room.

  2. Failed toilet wax ring

    The wax ring seals the toilet base to the flange. If it dries out, cracks, or the toilet rocks, gas and sometimes water leak at the base, often with a smell that is worst near the floor.

  3. Blocked or failed plumbing vent

    Roof vents let sewer gas escape and let drains breathe. A vent clogged by leaves, a nest, or ice, or one that was never properly installed, forces gas back through traps and causes gurgling drains.

  4. Cracked drain line or sewer lateral

    A cracked pipe inside a wall, under the slab, or in the yard lets gas seep out. In older Sonoma homes, cracked clay laterals both leak gas and invite root intrusion.

  5. Sewage ejector or sump pit issues

    Homes with a basement bathroom or below-grade fixtures use an ejector pit that must be sealed and vented. A bad seal, stuck check valve, or failed vent lets odor into the space.

  6. Biofilm or debris buildup

    A slimy bacterial buildup inside drains and disposals, or trapped hair and gunk, can produce a foul smell even when the trap is intact; this is usually the mildest and easiest to fix.

How to check it yourself

  1. Walk the house and try to localize the smell by room and by height; a floor-level smell near a toilet suggests a wax ring, while a smell at a sink suggests its trap.
  2. For any rarely used drain, sink, shower, or floor drain, run water for a minute to refill the P-trap; a small amount of mineral oil after refilling slows future evaporation.
  3. Check whether a toilet rocks or wobbles when you gently shift it, and look for discoloration or dampness at the base; either points to a failing wax ring or loose flange bolts.
  4. Clean drains and the garbage disposal (baking soda and hot water, or a disposal cleaner) to rule out biofilm before assuming a bigger problem.
  5. Notice if the smell is worse in wind or when fixtures drain, and whether drains gurgle; that pattern suggests a blocked roof vent.
  6. Do NOT go into a confined basement or crawlspace if you smell strong gas; sewer gas can displace oxygen. Ventilate the area and step outside for fresh air.
  7. If you ever smell natural gas (a separate added sulfur odor) or feel dizzy or nauseated, leave and call your gas utility or 911; do not treat that as a plumbing issue.

When to call a licensed plumber

Call a pro if the smell persists after you have refilled every trap and cleaned the drains, if a toilet rocks or leaks at the base, if multiple drains gurgle (a vent or main-line sign), or if you suspect a cracked pipe under the slab or in the yard. Get help urgently if the odor is strong and persistent in a basement, crawlspace, or other enclosed space, since concentrated sewer gas is an asphyxiation and, in rare cases, explosion risk. A pro can smoke-test the system to pinpoint hidden leaks and camera-inspect the lateral.

How to prevent it

  • Run water in guest bathrooms, floor drains, and utility sinks at least monthly to keep traps full.
  • Add a few ounces of mineral oil to rarely used floor drains to slow evaporation of the trap seal.
  • Keep roof vents clear; have them checked when you clean gutters.
  • Fix a rocking toilet promptly so the wax ring stays sealed.
  • Clean drains and disposals periodically to prevent odor-causing biofilm.
  • In older homes, have the sewer lateral camera-inspected periodically to catch cracks before they leak gas or admit roots.

Frequently asked questions

Is sewer gas in my house dangerous?

Low, occasional levels are mostly an unpleasant nuisance, but sewer gas contains hydrogen sulfide and methane. Higher concentrations can cause headaches and nausea, displace oxygen in enclosed spaces, and methane is flammable, so you should find and fix the source rather than just mask the smell.

Why does my guest bathroom smell only sometimes?

It is almost certainly a dry P-trap. When the bathroom sits unused, the water sealing the trap evaporates and lets gas in; running the water for a minute refills the trap and the smell disappears.

The smell is strongest near the toilet base. What is it?

That usually means a failed or dried wax ring or a toilet that rocks and has broken its seal. Water and gas escape at the floor line. Resetting the toilet with a new wax ring typically fixes it.

Can a blocked vent really cause odors?

Yes. If the roof vent is blocked, draining fixtures pull air through the traps instead, siphoning the water seal and letting gas into the home. You will often notice gurgling drains along with the smell.
