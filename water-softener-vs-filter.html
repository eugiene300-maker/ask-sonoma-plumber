Water Softener vs Filtration: Which Do You Need?

CL Reviewed by Cody Lehman · Updated August 2026

[ Water Quality · Sonoma County ]
Answer

A water softener and a filtration system solve different problems. A softener uses ion exchange to remove the calcium and magnesium that cause hard-water scale, while a filtration system removes contaminants, sediment, chlorine, and bad taste or odor. If your main issue is scale on fixtures and short appliance life, you need a softener; if it is taste, sediment, or specific contaminants, you need filtration. Many Sonoma County homes with hard water and well or aesthetic concerns end up wanting both.

Most likely causes

  1. Softeners target hardness

    Water softeners run water through resin that swaps hardness minerals for sodium (or potassium). This stops scale on fixtures, protects water heaters and appliances, and reduces spotting and soap scum. It does not remove most contaminants or improve taste much.

  2. Filtration targets contaminants and taste

    Filtration covers a broad range: carbon filters for chlorine taste and odor, sediment filters for grit, and reverse osmosis or specialty media for contaminants like iron, sulfur, or others. Filters do not soften water or stop scale.

  3. Why the two get confused

    Both improve water quality, so people assume one does the other's job. In reality, a softener will not remove a sulfur smell or sediment, and a basic filter will not stop hard-water scale.

How to decide what you need

  1. Name the problem: scale, spotting, and dry skin point to hardness (softener); bad taste, odor, cloudiness, or a contaminant concern point to filtration.
  2. Test your water for hardness and for anything specific like iron, sulfur, or chlorine.
  3. If hardness is your only complaint, start with a softener.
  4. If taste, sediment, or contaminants are the issue and water is not hard, start with filtration.
  5. If you have both hard water and taste or contaminant concerns (common with Sonoma wells), plan a combined system: a softener plus point-of-use or whole-home filtration.

How to prevent it

  • Test water before buying equipment so you treat the real problem.
  • Match filter type to the contaminant; a carbon filter will not fix sulfur odor.
  • Keep softener salt topped up and replace filter cartridges on schedule.
  • Consider a point-of-use RO filter for drinking water even if you soften the whole house.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need both a softener and a filter?

Often yes in Sonoma County. If your water is hard and also has taste, sediment, iron, or sulfur issues (common on wells), a softener handles the scale while filtration handles the rest. On city water with only hardness, a softener alone may be enough, with a small under-sink filter for drinking water.

Will a whole-home filter soften my water?

No. Standard carbon and sediment filters improve taste and remove particles but do not remove calcium and magnesium, so they will not stop scale. Only a softener (or a salt-free conditioner, which reduces scale) addresses hardness.

Can one system do everything?

Some combination units pair softening resin with filtration media in staged tanks. They can be a good fit for well water with multiple issues, but they cost more and need correct sizing and maintenance.

Which should I install first?

It depends on your worst complaint. If fixtures are crusting with scale, prioritize the softener. If your water tastes or smells bad, prioritize filtration. A plumber can sequence them correctly when you install both.
