Tank vs Tankless Water Heater: Which Is Right?

CL Reviewed by Cody Lehman · Updated August 2026

[ Costs & Decisions · Sonoma County ]
Answer

For most Sonoma and Marin households the right choice comes down to how you use hot water and how long you plan to stay in the home. A tank (storage) water heater costs far less upfront and is simpler to service, making it the practical pick for many homeowners. A tankless unit costs more to install but lasts longer, saves energy, and never runs out of hot water, so it pays off best for households that plan to stay put for a decade or more, or that run out of hot water with a conventional tank. Whichever you choose, our local hard water makes regular descaling maintenance essential.

How to decide between tank and tankless

  1. Estimate your peak demand: count how many showers, plus the dishwasher and laundry, might run at the same time. High simultaneous demand may need a larger tankless unit or two units.
  2. Check your fuel and infrastructure: gas tankless units often require a larger gas line and new venting, which adds to install cost. An electric tankless whole-home unit may need a significant panel upgrade.
  3. Factor in how long you will stay: the energy savings and longer lifespan of tankless pay back best over 10+ years of ownership.
  4. Account for hard water: budget for annual descaling either way, and strongly consider a softener or scale filter to protect a tankless unit's heat exchanger.
  5. Compare total lifetime cost, not just sticker price: a tankless costs more now but lasts roughly twice as long and uses less energy each month.
  6. Get a load-appropriate sizing quote from a licensed plumber before buying; an undersized unit of either type will disappoint.

When to call a licensed plumber

Water heater installation involves gas, venting, water connections, and California permit and seismic-strapping requirements, so it is not a beginner DIY job. Call a licensed plumber to size the unit, handle the gas or electrical work safely, pull permits, and ensure code-compliant venting. A pro can also tell you whether your gas line or electrical panel can support a tankless before you commit.

How to prevent it

  • Flush your water heater on a yearly schedule; our hard water builds sediment and scale faster than in soft-water regions.
  • For tankless, use the manufacturer's descaling procedure or an isolation valve kit so a plumber can service it quickly.
  • Set the thermostat around 120 degrees F to save energy and reduce scald risk and scale buildup.
  • Consider a water softener or scale-reduction filter to dramatically extend the life of either type of heater.
  • Insulate the first few feet of hot and cold pipes at the heater to cut standby heat loss.

Frequently asked questions

Does a tankless water heater really save money?

It saves energy every month, the DOE estimates 24 to 34 percent more efficient for typical homes, but the higher install cost means the payback comes over years. Households that keep the home long-term and use a lot of hot water see the best return.

Will a tankless unit run out of hot water?

It will not run out based on time the way a tank does, but it has a maximum flow rate. If too many fixtures draw hot water at once, the temperature can drop. Proper sizing, or a second unit, solves this.

Is hard water harder on tankless heaters?

Yes. Scale builds up on the heat exchanger and reduces efficiency and lifespan, which is why annual descaling is critical in Sonoma County. A softener or scale filter is a smart pairing with any tankless unit here.

Which lasts longer?

Tankless units typically last around 20 years versus 10 to 12 for a storage tank, but only with the required annual maintenance. Skip the descaling and that advantage shrinks quickly.
