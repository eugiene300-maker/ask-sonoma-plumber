Water Heaters

ProblemWhy Is My Water Heater Leaking?A leaking water heater usually comes from one of five places: the temperature and pressure relief (T&P) valve, the drain valve at the bottom, the tank connections, condensation, or the steel tank itself. Water dripping from a valve or fitting is often a repairable fix, but water seeping from the base of the tank almost always means internal corrosion and the unit needs replacement. Shut off the water and, for gas units, the gas; then trace the source before deciding what to do next.Read answer ↗ProblemWhy Do I Have No Hot Water?No hot water usually traces to a specific failed part. On a gas heater it is most often a pilot light or thermocouple that has gone out, or the gas valve; on an electric heater it is a tripped breaker, a failed heating element, or a bad thermostat. If you have some hot water that runs out fast, the likely culprits are sediment, an undersized tank, or a broken dip tube. Check the simple items first, and call a pro for gas valve, wiring, or element work.Read answer ↗MaintenanceHow to Flush & Maintain Your Water HeaterTo flush a tank water heater, turn off the power or gas, let the water cool, shut the cold-water supply, connect a hose to the drain valve, and drain the tank into a safe location, then briefly reopen the cold supply to stir up and rinse out sediment before refilling. In Sonoma County's hard water, flush once a year at minimum and ideally every six months, since minerals build up faster here. While you're at it, check the anode rod every few years, test the T&P valve, and set the thermostat to 120 F.Read answer ↗ProblemWhy Does My Hot Water Smell Like Rotten Eggs?A rotten-egg smell from your hot water is hydrogen sulfide gas, usually produced when sulfate-reducing bacteria in the water heater react with the tank's sacrificial anode rod. If the smell is only on the hot side, the water heater is almost always the source, and the fix is to flush and disinfect the tank and swap the standard magnesium anode for an aluminum/zinc or a powered anode. If both hot and cold water smell, the problem is more likely in the well or water supply and needs different treatment.Read answer ↗

Drains & Sewer

ProblemWhy Is My Drain Clogged (and How to Clear It)?A clogged drain is almost always caused by something physically narrowing or blocking the pipe: hair and soap scum in tubs and showers, grease and food in kitchen sinks, or non-flushable items in toilets. If just one fixture drains slowly, the clog is local and you can usually clear it yourself with a plunger, hot water, or a hand snake. If every drain in the house is slow or backing up at the same time, the problem is likely in your main sewer line, which needs a pro.Read answer ↗ProblemWhat Causes a Sewer Line Backup?A sewer line backup happens when your home's main drain line, the single pipe that carries all waste to the city sewer or septic, gets blocked. In older Sonoma County neighborhoods the number-one cause is tree roots growing into cracks and joints of aging clay sewer laterals. Grease buildup, flushed wipes, a sagging or collapsed pipe, and occasionally a problem in the city main are the other main causes. The telltale sign is that multiple fixtures back up at once and the lowest drain in the house floods when you run water elsewhere.Read answer ↗ProblemWhat Causes a Sewage Smell Indoors?A sewage or rotten-egg smell indoors means sewer gas is escaping into your home instead of venting out the roof. The most common cause is a dried-out P-trap under a rarely used sink, shower, or floor drain, which lets gas rise up the pipe. Other causes are a failed wax ring under a toilet, a blocked or damaged plumbing vent, a cracked drain or sewer lateral, or a problem sump/ejector pit. Sewer gas contains methane and hydrogen sulfide, so while low levels are mainly a nuisance, you should find and fix the source promptly.Read answer ↗MaintenanceHow to Prevent Clogged DrainsMost clogs are preventable with a few simple habits: keep grease and food out of the drain, put a screen over every drain to catch hair and scraps, and flush your drains with hot water regularly. Flush only toilet paper down the toilet, use enzyme cleaners instead of caustic chemicals for maintenance, and if you own an older Sonoma County home with a clay lateral, add root control to your routine. These low-cost steps prevent the buildup that leads to slow drains and expensive main-line backups.Read answer ↗

Pipes, Pressure & Fixtures

ProblemWhy Is My Water Pressure Low?Low water pressure usually comes from one of two places: a restriction at a single fixture (a scaled-up aerator or clogged cartridge) or a whole-house problem (a failing pressure-reducing valve, a partly closed shutoff, corroded galvanized pipe, or a clogged softener or filter). The fastest way to narrow it down is to ask whether the weak flow affects one fixture or the entire home. In many older Sonoma County homes, decades of hard-water scale and corroding galvanized pipe are the underlying cause.Read answer ↗ProblemWhy Does My Toilet Keep Running?A toilet that keeps running is almost always caused by something in the tank not sealing or shutting off: a worn or misaligned flapper, a chain that is too short or too long, a float set too high, or a fill valve that will not stop. The good news is that most running toilets are an easy, low-cost DIY fix, usually a new flapper. Start with a dye test to confirm where the water is escaping, then replace the failed part.Read answer ↗ProblemWhy Are My Pipes Banging? (Water Hammer)A loud bang, thud, or hammering when you shut off a faucet or when an appliance closes a valve is usually water hammer: a fast-moving column of water slamming to a stop and sending a shock wave through your pipes. It commonly shows up when high water pressure, missing or dead water-hammer arrestors, and loose pipes combine. The banging is not just annoying; the repeated shock can loosen joints and fittings over time, so it is worth diagnosing.Read answer ↗ProblemSigns You Have a Hidden Slab LeakA slab leak is a leak in a water line running under your home's concrete foundation. Tell-tale signs include an unexplained jump in your water bill, a warm or damp spot on the floor, the sound of running water when everything is off, low pressure, and unexplained cracks or mildew. You can screen for it with a simple water-meter test: shut off all water and see if the meter still moves. Because a hidden slab leak can undermine your foundation, it is worth confirming and fixing promptly.Read answer ↗

Water Quality

ProblemWhy Is My Water Brown or Rusty?Brown, yellow, or rusty water is almost always caused by iron and sediment getting stirred into your supply, and it is usually a nuisance rather than a health emergency. The key clues are whether it comes from hot water only, cold only, or both, and whether it affects one fixture or the whole house. Hot-only points to your water heater; all fixtures suddenly discolored often points to a disturbed municipal main or hydrant flushing; and slowly worsening discoloration in an older home can mean corroding galvanized pipes.Read answer ↗MaintenanceHard Water in Sonoma County: Effects & FixesHard water is water carrying dissolved minerals, mainly calcium and magnesium, and much of Sonoma County has moderately hard to hard water. The minerals themselves are not a health hazard, but they leave scale on fixtures, shorten the life of water heaters and appliances, spot your glassware, and can leave skin and hair feeling dry. The common fixes are a whole-home water softener, a salt-free conditioner, or targeted point-of-use filters, each suited to a different goal.Read answer ↗ComparisonWater Softener vs Filtration: Which Do You Need?A water softener and a filtration system solve different problems. A softener uses ion exchange to remove the calcium and magnesium that cause hard-water scale, while a filtration system removes contaminants, sediment, chlorine, and bad taste or odor. If your main issue is scale on fixtures and short appliance life, you need a softener; if it is taste, sediment, or specific contaminants, you need filtration. Many Sonoma County homes with hard water and well or aesthetic concerns end up wanting both.Read answer ↗

Emergency & Safety

SafetyWhy Do I Smell Gas in My House? (What to Do Now)If you smell gas (a rotten-egg or sulfur odor added to natural gas), treat it as an emergency. Do not touch light switches, appliances, or anything electrical, do not use your phone inside, and do not light a match, candle, or flame. Get everyone, including pets, out of the house immediately and leave doors open as you go. From a safe distance outside, call 911 and PG&E's 24-hour gas emergency line at 1-800-743-5000, then have a licensed plumber inspect and repair the gas line before you go back in.Read answer ↗EmergencyBurst Pipe: What to Do Right NowShut off your main water supply immediately to stop the flooding, then open faucets to drain the remaining water from the pipes. If water is near outlets, the electrical panel, or appliances, shut off power to the affected area at the breaker. Contain and mop up water, take photos and video for your insurance claim, and call a licensed plumber right away. Turning the water off first is the single most important step to limit the damage.Read answer ↗EmergencyHow to Shut Off Your Water in an EmergencyYou have three places to shut off water, from closest to the leak outward: the fixture shutoff (a small valve under a sink or behind a toilet), the main house shutoff (where the water line enters the home, often near the water heater, front hose bib, or in the garage), and the street/meter shutoff at the curb box. For most leaks, close the nearest fixture valve first. For a burst pipe or a leak with no obvious fixture, go straight to the main house shutoff. Turn valves clockwise (righty-tighty) to close, and if the leak involves a water heater, also shut off its gas or power.Read answer ↗MaintenanceHow to Prevent Frozen & Burst PipesTo prevent frozen and burst pipes, insulate the pipes most exposed to cold, disconnect and drain garden hoses before winter, and during an actual freeze let a faucet drip and keep your home heated. Sonoma County's inland and valley areas, Santa Rosa, Windsor, Healdsburg, get occasional hard freezes, and older homes here often have vulnerable pipes in crawlspaces and exterior walls. Knowing where your main water shutoff is, before a pipe bursts, is the single most valuable step, because it lets you stop the flooding fast.Read answer ↗

Costs & Decisions

CostWater Heater Replacement Cost in Sonoma County (2026)In Sonoma County in 2026, expect roughly $1,800-$3,500 installed for a standard 40- or 50-gallon tank water heater, and about $4,500-$8,000 installed for a tankless unit. North Bay labor runs higher than national averages, and code upgrades such as an expansion tank, seismic strapping, and updated venting are often required and add to the total. Your final price depends mostly on fuel type, whether you switch technologies, and how much your existing setup needs to be brought up to current code.Read answer ↗CostHow Much Does Drain Cleaning Cost?In Sonoma County and the greater Bay Area in 2026, a straightforward drain snaking or auger job typically runs about $150 to $400. Hydro-jetting to blast out roots and grease runs roughly $500 to $1,200, and a video camera inspection runs about $250 to $500. Clearing a main line through an existing cleanout is on the lower end; if the plumber has to locate or dig to access the line, or work through a roof vent, costs climb. North Bay labor rates run above the national average, so local prices tend toward the higher end of these ranges.Read answer ↗CostSewer Line Repair & Replacement CostIn Sonoma County and the Bay Area in 2026, a small spot repair on a sewer lateral typically runs about $2,500 to $6,000, while a full lateral replacement runs roughly $8,000 to $25,000 or more depending on length, depth, and access. Trenchless methods (pipe lining or pipe bursting) often land in the $8,000 to $20,000 range and avoid tearing up your yard, driveway, or landscaping. Traditional open-trench digging can cost less per foot but adds restoration costs for hardscape and yard. Permits, depth, and whether the line runs under a driveway or the street heavily influence the final number, and North Bay labor runs above the national average.Read answer ↗CostHow Much Does a Whole-Home Repipe Cost?A whole-home repipe in Sonoma and Marin typically runs about $6,000 to $18,000, with most single-story homes landing near $8,000 to $12,000. PEX is usually the more affordable choice and copper the pricier, longer-lived option. Your final number depends mostly on the number of bathrooms, whether the home sits on a slab or a crawlspace, and how much drywall has to be opened and repaired afterward.Read answer ↗DecisionShould You Repair or Replace Your Water Heater?Use this simple rule: replace the water heater if it's older than 8-12 years, the tank itself is leaking, you've had repeated repairs, or a repair would cost more than half the price of a new unit; otherwise repair it if the problem is a single minor part and the unit is still young. A leaking tank is never worth repairing. When a heater is near the end of its expected life, putting money into it rarely pays off, and a new efficient unit usually makes more sense.Read answer ↗DecisionDo You Need a Repipe? Signs to WatchYou likely need a whole-home repipe if you have repeated pinhole leaks, rusty or brown water from multiple faucets, chronic low pressure, or confirmed galvanized steel or polybutylene pipe, especially in a home that is 40-plus years old. A single isolated leak is usually a repair; a pattern of failures across the house is the signal to repipe. When two or more of these signs show up together, it is time to have a licensed plumber evaluate the whole system.Read answer ↗ComparisonTank vs Tankless Water Heater: Which Is Right?For most Sonoma and Marin households the right choice comes down to how you use hot water and how long you plan to stay in the home. A tank (storage) water heater costs far less upfront and is simpler to service, making it the practical pick for many homeowners. A tankless unit costs more to install but lasts longer, saves energy, and never runs out of hot water, so it pays off best for households that plan to stay put for a decade or more, or that run out of hot water with a conventional tank. Whichever you choose, our local hard water makes regular descaling maintenance essential.Read answer ↗
